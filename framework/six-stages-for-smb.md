# The six stages, for a small business

The [six-stage model](https://github.com/VictorOsondu/ai-adoption-model) sorts people by how AI adoption *feels*, not by tools deployed. Here's what each stage looks like inside a small or medium business — a team of roughly 10 to 200 people — and the one thing that tends to move it forward.

In an SMB the stages usually apply twice: to the **owner or leader**, and to the **team**. The two often sit at different stages, and the gap between them is where most adoption stalls.

Last reviewed: 2026-07-24. See [sources and verification notes](../sources.md).

| Stage | In an SMB it looks like… | What moves it |
|-------|--------------------------|---------------|
| **Nomad** | "We're too small / too busy / too regulated for this." AI is a distraction from real work. | A single concrete win in a function they already care about — a quote drafted in a minute, a policy summarised. |
| **Observer** | The owner reads about it; a couple of staff quietly use ChatGPT at home. Nothing official. | Permission to try, on low-stakes work, without fear of getting it wrong or breaking a rule. |
| **Dabbler** | Scattered, personal use. One person automates their emails; nobody else knows. Wins don't spread. | A shared, repeatable use case and a ground rule so people know what's allowed. |
| **Integrator** | AI is part of how several functions work. Time is genuinely saved. But it's individual, undocumented, and uneven. | A light policy + the readiness to make it a company standard, not a set of private habits. |
| **Architect** | The owner can see a bigger play — a service, a product, an automated workflow — but hasn't committed to building it. | The self-permission to build for customers, not just to run the office faster. |
| **Innovator** | The business builds AI into what it sells or how it operates, and it's a visible advantage. | Choosing which problem is worth the investment — and governing it as you scale. |

## Why the owner–team gap matters

The most common SMB pattern: an **Integrator owner** and a **Nomad/Observer team**, or the reverse — enthusiastic staff and a cautious owner. The skill isn't the problem. Adoption stalls because the two halves of the business are answering different questions.

- If the **team is ahead of the owner**, the risk is ungoverned use — client data in public tools, no policy, no owner, no review trail. The fix is a ground rule and a light policy, fast.
- If the **owner is ahead of the team**, the risk is a tool nobody adopts. The fix is a shared win and visible permission, not a mandate.

## The ICE Method for an SMB

Where to actually spend the effort, three ways:

- **Improve** — the non-sensitive admin and comms that eat owner and manager time: meeting notes, internal summaries, first-draft policies, public research, draft outlines. Fastest return, lowest risk. Start here.
- **Create** — things the business couldn't easily make before: a knowledge base, customer FAQs, campaign variants, structured reports.
- **Educate** — the literacy that keeps it safe: what AI gets wrong, what data never goes in, and when a human must stay in the loop. In a regulated or client-serving SMB this isn't optional — it's the difference between a time-saver and an incident.

## The safe-use line

Small businesses often handle the same kinds of sensitive data as large ones with fewer people to catch mistakes. Client contracts, personal data, employee records, financial records, credentials, customer data, proprietary code, health or safeguarding information, and anything confidential must not go into a public AI tool. Approved work tools still need clear settings, owners, data limits, and review. The [readiness assessment](https://github.com/VictorOsondu/ai-adoption-toolkit/blob/main/assessment/readiness-assessment.md) checks whether that line is actually in place before you scale.
